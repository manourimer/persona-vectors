Persona Vectors for Moral Character — Manou Rimer

Do AI models have four
moral compasses,
or just one?

This project built four separate instruments to measure honesty, harmlessness, fairness, and compassion inside a language model — then found the four instruments were secretly reading the same needle.

Persona vectors are being proposed as a lightweight way to monitor AI systems for concerning behaviour during deployment. This is a stress test of that idea for moral character specifically — and a look at where it holds up and where it doesn't.

01 / 09
The Idea

A "persona vector" is a dial
for a specific trait in a model

Prompting a model to act very honest, then very dishonest, causes its internal activations shift along a specific, findable direction. That direction is the trait; turning it one way the model leans honest, turning it the other way it leans dishonest (Chen et al., 2025).

These vectors are appealing: a lightweight dial for monitoring a deployed model's behaviour. This project asked whether it works for moral character; building one dial each for honesty, harmlessness, fairness, and compassion, expecting four independent readings.

02 / 09
Method, Briefly

How each dial gets built and tested

  • ModelGemma-3-12B-IT — all analysis on residual-stream activations at layers 32, 40, 47 (of 48 blocks).
  • Build a dial5 contrastive system prompts per pole ("deeply honest AI" vs. "deceptive AI") × 40 elicitation questions, scored by a judge model, filtered to high-confidence responses. The dial = the difference between average internal states under the two poles.
  • Test each dialProject onto 204 real moral scenarios (ETHICS benchmark) — plus a second, independent 160-item set written from scratch to remove a confound in the first (next slide).
  • "Collapse," measuredEffective dimensionality: a number from 1–4 saying how many genuinely independent directions the four dials' readings span. 4 = fully independent; 1 = one dial in disguise.

Finding One

Four dials turned out
to be one dial

1.13
effective dimensionality at layer 32 — out of a possible 4
2.46
at layer 40 — the one layer with partial separation
1.15
at layer 47 — barely more than one dimension

We expected a scenario high on honesty to be roughly neutral on compassion. Instead, across 204 real moral scenarios, all four readings moved almost perfectly together.

04 / 09
Finding One — Robustness Check

Was this just messy data?
A second test, same result.

ETHICS has a flaw: item format is entangled with trait label (deontology items are 96% honesty; justice items are 72% fairness). So we wrote an independent 160-item bank — one format, all four traits, zero shared vocabulary — and reran everything.

05 / 09
Finding Two

The obvious explanation:
maybe it's more generic than morality

Maybe all four "moral" dials are secretly re-detecting one simpler thing learned in training: a generic "be a good AI" vs. "be a bad AI" feeling, wearing four different outfits.

This is directly testable by building a fifth dial — deliberately generic, no mention of honesty, harm, fairness, or compassion at all.

What was found first
This generic "virtuous" dial was extremely well-defined on its own — separating virtuous from unethical responses even more cleanly than any of the four moral dials (up to 100% held-out accuracy). A generic good/bad signal is real and easy to find.

So What

A reliable instrument —
pointed at the wrong resolution

What holds up
The shared axis is real, replicates independently, and is measured reliably across paraphrases. Good enough to flag "something is off" during deployment monitoring.
What doesn't
You cannot currently use this method to certify a model as specifically "honest" or specifically "fair." It doesn't cut moral character at the joints we assumed — and the obvious reason why has been ruled out.

The tool works. It just isn't measuring what we — or Chen et al.'s per-trait framing of persona vectors — assumed it was. For anyone building safety evals on persona vectors: treat a single trait score as a general moral-salience alarm, not proof of a specific virtue. Why the axis collapses is still the open question.

Chen et al. build one persona vector per named trait (e.g. evil, sycophancy) and use each to monitor that trait specifically — while noting in a footnote, but not systematically testing, that shifts across traits "tend to shift together." Chen, R., Arditi, A., Sleight, H., Evans, O., & Lindsey, J. (2025). Persona Vectors: Monitoring and Controlling Character Traits in Language Models. arXiv:2507.21509, fn. 6.
